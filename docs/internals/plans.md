# Plans

Every change to a workload's declared state passes through a plan: computed, persisted, inspectable
and approvable before anything happens. This one object yields dry-run, approval, scheduled changes,
rollback and honest audit, and is what makes a chat surface safe to hold authority: a bot posts the
plan and its impact, someone with the right grant approves, and nobody holds standing authority to
mutate production from a chat message.

## The plan

```
Plan
  workload, kind      intent | power | retire | migrate | remake | delete_volume |
                      snapshot_volume | rollback_volume | delete_snapshot | backup_volume |
                      restore_volume | prune_backups
  operation           the parent operation, when generated by one
  from_intent, from_power   the target and power target it was computed against
  to_intent, to_power, to_volume, to_repository   by kind
  computed_at, computed_by, proposed_under (the grant that permitted the declaration)
  steps               index, action, executor, target, reversible, timeout, disruptive,
                      classified_by, reason, detail
  impact              disruptive, downtime estimate, data at risk, cost delta, each endpoint's
                      exposure, and why it does or does not auto-approve
  status              proposed | approved | queued | applying | applied | failed | cancelled |
                      rejected | expired | superseded
  completed           the last step whose completion was ingested
  apply_after         approved now, applied in a window
  expires_at, approved_by, approved_at
  approval            auto | confirmation | authority, and the approval scope
```

Steps are keyed by plan and index, unique by construction. Each step that stops a running object
carries `stops_within_seconds`: the recipe's pre-stop wait and the node's thirty-second grace, the
longest a stop may take before the process is killed. Downtime as a whole stays unknown, since how
long software takes to come back is measured nowhere.

## Rules

- **Every change to declared state is a plan**: an intent, a power change, a retirement, a
  migration, a volume operation. No code path changes what a node is assigned without one.
- **A plan is computed against a specific state.** If reality moves underneath it, it is superseded
  and recomputed; only a plan for the head is approvable, and a newer declaration supersedes every
  unapproved plan for an older one. Plans expire.
- **Steps are ordered, idempotent and resumable.** A failed or cancelled plan is resumable from its
  last completed step; resources its completed steps made are kept and named, never orphaned or
  deleted on the assumption they are unwanted. The workload shows as partially applied, a fact of
  the plan held by the control plane, never an observed state. Creating one workload is already
  three durable side effects (a volume, an endpoint, a runtime object), so this is what makes
  ordinary creation, not only migration, resumable and reversible.
- **An intent plan's steps are computed from the last intent the workload reached**, not from the
  target approval already moved: the target, unless the plan approved to reach it failed, in which
  case the intent that plan started from. Steps a failed plan completed are planned again and
  complete as no-ops, and an install resumes at the action that failed, so declaring the same body
  again is how a person resumes a failed plan.
- **The guarantee reaches inside a step.** "Run the install" is one plan step and several install
  actions; resuming at the plan step would refetch forty gigabytes over a directory deliberately
  kept. So install actions are ordered, individually idempotent and resumable, with progress
  recorded per install, and the install language's verbs are a closed set so each can state its
  idempotence.
- **A retire plan moves no pointer, and approving it freezes the workload.** Deleting a workload
  proposes: stop, detach and keep every volume, remove the runtime, then delete, each by its own
  irreversible step, only the volumes the deletion named. Removing a runtime is disruptive by the
  vocabulary's floor, so a person always approves. Once approved, every other proposal is superseded
  and nothing else may be declared until it finishes or is cancelled. The name and quota are
  released when the node reports the runtime removed, or at approval for a workload no node holds.
- **A step marked irreversible** (deleting a volume, releasing an address) always needs explicit
  approval, and a plan whose impact is unknown is never auto-approved.

## What approval a plan needs

- **Auto**: every step reversible and non-disruptive and the impact known. Applied at once; the plan
  records its proposer as approver under the grant that permitted the declaration, and the effect
  says it was approved by default policy, claiming no other subject's authority. Pressing Start is a
  power declaration whose plan is auto-approved in the same call: one click for the user, a complete
  declaration, plan and effect record for the system. The plan is a receipt, not a round trip.
- **Confirmation**: the proposer's grant permits the action, but the plan is not auto-approvable or
  its proposer asked to confirm it (`korpis apply --plan`). The proposer may confirm: approval asks
  that a person see the change, and the proposer reading the plan is that person. Confirming
  re-evaluates the proposer's authority.
- **Authority**: proposed under a grant with `requires_approval_by`, it needs another subject
  holding `plan.approve` over the approval scope, by the rules on [grants](grants.md); the proposer
  can never supply it.

## Where disruptive comes from

"Auto-approved if non-disruptive" would be a policy engine hidden in an adjective, and the adjective
is not a property of the step: stopping a game server with forty players is disruptive, stopping a
stateless web service behind a load balancer is routine. So classification has one source and one
override chain, both recorded in the plan:

| Layer | Declares | Over |
|---|---|---|
| the action vocabulary | a floor: deleting a volume is disruptive and irreversible, always | nothing; it cannot be relaxed |
| the driver | how a tier changes it: a stop on a VM costs a boot | the floor, upward only |
| the recipe | what this software costs to interrupt | the driver, upward only |
| the operator, per workload | a deliberate override, recorded as an effect | everything above the floor |

Every layer may only make a step more disruptive than the one beneath, except the operator, who may
relax the driver and recipe but never the floor. That asymmetry is the safety property: no recipe
author or driver argues the floor away, and an operator wanting quieter approvals says so on the
record. Each step records which layer decided it and why. The operator's layer is
`SetClassification`, replaced whole under the workload's version with `workload.classify` and a
required reason; an entry that would relax the floor is refused rather than stored and ignored. A
plan already proposed keeps the classification its proposer saw. The driver's layer is the union of
what every node serving the organization and offering the tier declares.

## Plans govern declarations, not drift correction

The reconciler runs continuously: a crashed process is restarted, a vanished container recreated, a
drifted firewall rule rewritten. A plan per repair would bury the audit trail in noise; no record at
all would make two mutation paths. They are different operations:

| | Produces a plan | Authorized by |
|---|---|---|
| a declaration: a subject declares something new | always | the subject's grant |
| drift correction: reality diverged from what was approved | never | the approval already given |

Converging toward an approved state executes a decision already made and audited. Every restart,
recreation and repair is still an effect.

## Effects

```
Effect
  plan, step, workload, node
  object, organization  what it is about, and the organization whose subtree holds it
  actor, relay          who acted, and the client that relayed it
  grant                 the grant that authorized it
  at                    when the control plane ingested it: the only ordering clock
  occurred_at           the originating node's reading, informational
  origin                node and journal sequence, for node effects; unique
  action, outcome       succeeded | failed | skipped | rolled_back | denied
  error, before, after
  count, window_end     repeats coalesced into it
```

Effects are append-only, never updated or deleted, retained by archival. Every effect names the
grant that authorized it, so "under what authority, delegated from whom" is answerable. The control
plane's own effects (a placement, a lease, a step a node executed) name the grant of the decision
they carry out; a denial names its subject and the missing action. Audit is not a parallel log that
can drift from reality: the effect stream is the record. See [state](state.md) for how effects are
stored and [observability](observability.md) for how they are read.

## Operations

A plan covers one workload. Draining a node touches every workload on it, and approving two hundred
plans one by one is not a workflow anyone uses.

```
Operation
  kind       drain | evacuate | migrate | rebalance | recipe_rollout | bulk_intent |
             node_upgrade | template_rollout
  scope      a node, a template version, a selector or an explicit set
  items      one per workload or grant in scope, each generating its plan as it begins
  policy     concurrency, max_unavailable, on_failure: pause | continue | abort, start_after
  status     proposed | approved | running | paused | completed | failed | aborted
  progress   total, waiting, applying, settling, done, failed, skipped
```

An operation owns many plans with one approval, one progress view and one pause, resume and abort
control. Each plan is still computed against its own workload and audited, names its operation, and
the person approves the decision actually made ("drain node 7"), not two hundred restatements.
Plans are generated as the operation proceeds, since a plan computed two hours earlier would be
superseded on arrival.

**Items are fixed at proposal and found again at approval.** Proposing finds every item in scope
and keeps it with what it needs, so the proposer and the approver read the same list; approving
finds them again, adding an item that arrived and skipping one that left. Nothing arrives after:
a drained or evacuated node goes `draining` as the operation is approved, and takes no placement,
and its end, however it ends, leaves the node `cordoned`. A node is under one operation that has
not ended at a time, which the store enforces.

**Approval is a person's, and the control plane acts as them.** Proposing and deciding take the
action that governs the kind: `node.drain` or `node.evacuate` on the node, and for a rollout
`template.write` on the template's organization, as editing the template does, with `grant.read`
there too, since every answer about one lists the grants it is over and their holders; reading an
operation takes reading its node, or `grant.read` there. Approving takes a person
acting directly, outside a portal: the operation records who approved, under which grant, and
whether their credential carried a second factor, and each item begins as that caller, with that
authority decided again as it begins. What reached the approver through a relay or a portal session
is not there when the item begins, so it is refused rather than reconstructed. An approver who lost
the authority since begins nothing more; that item fails, as the policy says.

**The reconciler admits by the policy, every pass.** The control plane's loop, each heartbeat
interval, starts each approved operation whose `start_after` has come, settles each item under way
against what nodes report, ends the operation once every item has (completed if none failed, failed
otherwise), and begins as many waiting items as the policy admits, each in a transaction of its own
under the operation's lock, so a pause or an abort decided meanwhile is seen before the next one.
An item that ends as it begins, as a rollout's grant does, takes no place, so the pass goes on to
the next and ends the operation once every item has, rather than one grant a heartbeat.
An item applies while its plan does; one that moved a workload then settles until the node it now
runs on reports it running, or stopped as its power says. `concurrency` bounds the items applying,
and `max_unavailable` the items applying or settling, since any applying item may stop its workload
and one that has not been seen running since is down. A workload that fails to start where it lands
holds the budget, which is what the bound is for: without it every workload on the node migrates at
once, a self-inflicted outage. An item that cannot begin (no node takes it, it migrates already, its
grant was revoked) fails alone with the refusal's reason, and the operation pauses, continues or
fails as `on_failure` says; a fault of the control plane's own fails nothing and is tried at the
next pass. An abort begins nothing more and skips what waits; a plan already applying goes on and is
the operator's to end through its own controls.

- **drain** moves each workload off a node that takes part, by a `migrate` plan toward the
  scheduler's choice, approved in the transaction that proposes it, which begins the migration as
  any approved migration begins. A workload deleted or moved off meanwhile is skipped. Its item
  settles once the migration has moved it and fails if the migration is abandoned or rolled back.
  The node then holds nothing but each migration's source copies until their holds end, and is
  removed as any cordoned node is.
- **evacuate** is drain for a node found unreachable: it places each workload again from its target,
  as failover does, but on an operator's decision, which accepts what failover never does on its
  own. Each volume comes from where another node reaches it; or from its freshest replica, whatever
  the declaration says of automatic failover, losing what was written since its last snapshot; or,
  its only copy lost with the node, it is made anew holding its latest backup, losing what was
  written since, or empty, losing everything. The lost volume is detached and kept as an orphan on
  that node. Each item names, at proposal and again as it begins, where each volume comes from and
  since when data is lost, and whether the workload may still be running there: a `keep_running`
  workload may, and a copy started elsewhere splits it, which is why the scheduler never does this
  on its own. A `stop` workload still waits for its lease to end, since two writers would corrupt a
  volume both reach. One whose plan is under way on the node, or whose declaration attaches a lost
  volume, is refused, since the node holds what that plan made and an attachment cannot be made
  anew. The remake plan names the operation and its approver. The new node acts only under a lease,
  issued once the old one is past its term at the grantor and under a higher epoch, which fences the
  old node should it return.
- **bulk_intent** ("restart these two hundred", "label these five hundred") gives per-item results:
  a workload changed underneath conflicts alone and the rest proceed. All-or-nothing across
  independently owned workloads would let one person's edit block everyone's change.
- **template_rollout** is an operation over grants: one approval for "give every Moderator what
  version 3 adds", an item per live grant issued from the template at an earlier version, each
  issuing what the version adds over the grant's own version as a grant of its own to the same
  holder, on the same scope and under the same conditions. The approver names a grant they hold, and
  each addition is attenuated from it through the ordinary check of an issue, so a rollout gives
  nobody what its approver could not. The grant added to is untouched, so revoking the addition takes
  back only what the rollout gave. A grant whose chain is not in force before its turn (it, or
  any grant it was attenuated from, revoked, suspended or expired) fails alone, and so does one
  limited in uses, whose remaining uses an addition could not share; one not in force when the
  rollout is proposed is not an item at all.
- **rebalance** is only ever asked for: the scheduler may recommend, never move a running workload
  on its own.

**What it costs.** An item's authority is the approver's, read again later, so an approval made
with a second factor stands for actions taken after that factor was presented; a person who does
not want that pauses or aborts. A drain holds each workload's source copies for a day by default,
so the node is removable only once the last hold ends, or at once by removing it, which completes
those migrations. Settling waits on a report, so a node that never reports a workload running holds
its operation's budget until a person aborts it.
